### Chapter Summary: Safe to Great — The New Psychology of Leadership by Skip Bowman

---

#### Introduction: The New Psychology of Leadership and Its Significance

The book *Safe to Great* by Skip Bowman introduces a transformative model for leadership in the context of today’s complex organizational and societal challenges. At its core is the concept of *psychological safety*—the shared belief that a team or organization is safe for interpersonal risk-taking—and *growth mindset*, adapted from Carol Dweck’s seminal work, emphasizing the belief that abilities and intelligence can be developed.

This new psychology of leadership is especially significant given global disruptors—*climate change*, *AI* and the *Globotic Age*, the *Caring Age* with shifting demographics and mental health concerns, and the *Digital Enlightenment Age*. These forces shape the demands placed on organizations and redefine leadership roles.

Relevant concepts include:

- **Psychological safety**: Employees feeling able to speak candidly without fear of punishment or humiliation.
- **Growth mindset**: The belief that through effort, strategy, and help from others, one can improve abilities and intelligence.
- **Protective mindsets**: Default leadership behaviors based on control, compliance, or cynicism that impede growth.
- **Commitment premium**: The enhanced organizational performance that committed employees produce in contrast to mere compliance driven by control.

Bowman’s model combines psychological safety and growth mindset into six *growth principles* that leaders should adopt, balanced against understanding and mitigating three *protective mindsets* that often dominate.

---

### Part I: The Bright Side of Leadership — Six Growth Principles

**1. The Commitment Premium**

- *Commitment* is a choice where people engage deeply, take initiative, and stay loyal; it yields better performance than mere *control*.
- *Dolphins* are the metaphor for growth-minded leaders: collaborative, empathetic, curious, and courageous.
- Real-world examples:  
  - **Paul O’Neill at Alcoa** transformed organizational culture by making safety a *keystone habit*, which improved productivity and morale.
  - **Bazball**, the England Men’s Test Cricket team’s new leadership style under Brendon McCullum and Ben Stokes, fostered a safe, playful environment, resulting in a dramatic turnaround.
  - **Satya Nadella at Microsoft** rewrote corporate culture by embedding learning, empathy, and innovation at the core.
- Psychological safety fosters *speaking up* which leads to learning and innovation.
- Statistics: Only 36–38% of U.S. employees report high levels of engagement or job satisfaction, strongly linked to leadership style.
- Control breeds compliance; autonomy fosters commitment and higher productivity.

**2. Transform**

- Leaders must articulate and embody a bold, meaningful *purpose* that transcends profit—including environmental sustainability and social responsibility.
- Purpose invigorates employees by connecting personal goals to a larger contribution.
- Example: **Greta Thunberg’s climate activism** as a powerful individual purpose fueling a global movement.
- Purpose-led organizations nurture ethical, responsible, and cross-boundary collaboration.

**3. Aim High**

- Leaders create clear, challenging but achievable goals balancing risk and ambition.
- Inspired by Apollo missions’ *fail-safe* engineering mindset, effective leaders provide structures for autonomy within clear guardrails.
- Intrinsic motivators like mastery and autonomy enhance engagement.
- Use *data transparency,* scoreboards, and participative accountability to sustain momentum.

**4. Explore**

- Innovation flourishes in cultures where people are encouraged to be curious, playful, and open to new ideas—including tolerating failure and learning from mistakes.
- Role model: **Pixar’s culture of serendipitous encounters, peer review (“brain trust”), and safe psychological environment**.
- Curiosity and humility are essential for continuous learning and adaptation.

**5. Go High**

- Leadership under pressure requires *resilience, grit, optimism,* and emotional resourcefulness.
- Leaders set the *emotional tone* that enables focus and positive energy amid adversity.
- Example: **Jacinda Ardern’s compassionate, courageous leadership** after the Christchurch mosque shootings.
- Emotional composure prevents toxic blame games and enables recovery.

**6. Lift Others Up**

- Helping others grow through coaching, mentoring, and recognizing contributions builds trust and engagement.
- Acknowledging the *social risks of learning* and providing relational support mitigates fears of incompetence.
- Example: **Siya Kolisi and the South African Springboks**, using personal story and leadership to inspire a nation.
- Encouragement facilitates learning, risk-taking, and boosts operational quality and safety.

**7. Team Up**

- Effective collaboration harnesses collective intelligence, creativity, and diverse perspectives.
- Modern work requires *teaming*—rapid, flexible, and inclusive cross-boundary collaboration, rather than stable long-term teams.
- Examples include global efforts during the COVID-19 pandemic and innovative “hot spots” where diversity is embraced.
- Empathy and openness to alternative views overcome natural preferences for homogeneity.
- Collaboration increases psychological safety, information sharing, and innovation.

**Summary of Part I**

The six growth principles form an integrated framework shifting leadership from control to commitment, leveraging autonomy, purpose, collaboration, and emotional engagement. Together, they create a *new psychology of leadership* essential for thriving in our turbulent and interconnected world.

### Part II: The Dark Side — Protective Mindsets That Undermine Growth

**Why Protective Mindsets Persist**

- Despite clear evidence supporting growth-oriented leadership, *controlling leadership* (the Hippo), *compliance/passivity* (the Snail), and *cynicism/skepticism* (the Clam) dominate many organizations.
- This dominance is explained by comfort zones, fear, and ingrained cultural and genetic predispositions.
- Protective mindsets offer emotional safety and often short-term thrills, but they degrade psychological safety, cause disengagement, and reduce effectiveness.
  
**9. Why Leaders Choose Control Over Commitment**

- Leaders often resort to control in response to uncertainty or threats.
- Classic organizational disasters like the 1987 *King’s Cross fire* show how controlling cultures standardize silence and undermine initiative, worsening crises.
- Protective mindsets suppress truthful communication, creating *conspiracies of silence* deadly for innovation and safety.
- Example: Boeing and the Deepwater Horizon disaster show how controlling leadership precipates catastrophic outcomes.
- *Diminishers*, described by Liz Wiseman, are controlling leaders who micromanage, hoard credit, and stunt team capabilities.
- Control breeds fear, shrinking speech and creativity.

**10. The Hippo**

- Hippos are aggressive, controlling, domineering leaders who thrive on power and induce fear.
- Their behavior—including bullying, unrealistic demands, and blame—damages trust, causes high turnover, and creates toxic workplaces.
- Example: *Rashid*, a CEO who publicly humiliates staff, triggering organizational dysfunction.
- Hippos often get rewarded and promoted despite harm they cause due to charisma, connections, and shareholder focus.
- *Pantomime of power*: Hippos use symbols and dominance to maintain control, often justifying unethical actions to protect their status.
- The *No Hippo Zone* campaign aims to reduce abusive leadership and toxicity—proving transformative cultural change is possible.

**11. The Snail**

- Snails are compliant, conflict-averse, indirect leaders who prioritize relationships over accountability.
- Their over-reliance on bureaucracy, fear of rejection, and hesitancy to act cause slow decision-making and ineffective leadership.
- They protect themselves via conformity, sacrificing innovation and courageous leadership.
- Example: *Marek*, who fails to honestly lead renegotiations, and whose hesitancy contributes to failure.
- Snails create workplaces low in psychological safety despite their outward niceness.
- They are vulnerable to exploitation by Hippos.

**12. The Clam**

- Clams are cynics: emotionally detached, skeptical leaders who resist change and actively undermine others.
- They erect barriers of criticism, distrust, and intellectual superiority, which isolate them and block teamwork.
- Example: *Tene*, a toxic compliance officer who is feared and avoided.
- Clams are resistant to feedback and change, making coaching difficult.
- They prefer safe, rigid structures, impeding innovation and collaboration.
- Resistance is fueled by a need for self-protection, creating passive-aggressive and controlling dynamics.
- While useful for critical assessment in moderation, Clams’ overemphasis on skepticism kills morale and progress.

**13. The Cost of Darkness**

- Protective mindsets cause silence, cover-ups, costly mistakes, conflict, poor mental health, and organizational resistance.
- Culture of fear suppresses speaking up, leading to hidden errors and catastrophic failures.
- Whistleblowers often face retaliation despite legal protections.
- Excessive conflict time in organizations drains billions in productivity annually.
- Toxic work environments cause stress, burnout, and degradation of trust in leadership.
  
**Summary of Part II**

Growth mindset principles remain uncommon because protective mindsets are deeply embedded and rewarding in organizations. Hippos induce fear and conflict; Snails breed indecision and passivity; Clams generate cynicism and resist change. True transformation requires recognizing, confronting, and shifting these protective behaviors toward growth.

### Part III: From Safe to Great — Personal and Organizational Transformation

**A New Psychology of Leadership**

- Leaders face unprecedented demands in the *Green, Globotic, Caring,* and *Digital Enlightenment* Ages.
- Complex questions arise about sustainability, AI integration, equity, and human-technology collaboration.
- Quick fixes do not work; transformation is a long-term process requiring leaders to visibly embody growth mindset principles.
- Real change requires confronting denial, complacency, and biases within organizations.

**Four-Phase Model of Growth Mindset Development**

1. **Awareness**  
   - Critical self-reflection to identify blind spots and the gap between self-perception and others’ views.
   - Recognize one’s role in organizational culture and dysfunction.
   - Example: *John* discovers he is perceived as a controlling Hippo despite self-perception as a growth-minded leader.

2. **Acceptance**  
   - Taking responsibility for personal shortcomings and committing to change.
   - Setting development goals and involving others for feedback.
   - John acknowledges a need to improve listening and adopt a more collaborative style.

3. **Action**  
   - Engaging in disciplined practice to develop new habits despite discomfort.
   - Sharing change intentions openly and soliciting candid feedback.
   - John learns to ask questions, delegate, and coach, overcoming the *consciously incompetent* phase.

4. **Acceleration**  
   - Embedding growth mindset as identity, teaching others, and changing organizational practices.
   - Sustaining communities committed to continuous growth and accountability.
   - John sustains changes, supports others, and advocates for culture shift within his company.

**The Three Paths for Leadership:**

- *Optimistic*: Embrace growth mindset principles superficially or selectively—a dangerous “mindset washing.”
- *Cynical*: Embrace control and exploitation as realpolitik, persisting inequality and toxic culture.
- *Courageous*: Embrace the difficult journey to genuinely transform, hold dark and bright sides in balance, and foster growth.

**Final Thoughts**

The Safe2Great leadership model calls on leaders to balance *challenge* and *support*, nurture *psychological safety*, create *purpose-driven ecosystems*, and overcome ingrained *protective mindsets*. The journey from safe to great confronts uncomfortable truths but offers hope for a more equitable, innovative, and resilient future.

### Key Takeaways and Implications

- Leadership effectiveness and organizational excellence depend critically on creating *psychological safety* and fostering a *growth mindset* culture.
- The six growth principles—*Transform, Aim High, Explore, Go High, Lift Others Up,* and *Team Up*—interlock to create dynamic, innovative, and committed workplaces.
- Protective mindsets—characterized metaphorically as Hippos, Snails, and Clams—dominate many organizations and must be acknowledged and addressed explicitly.
- Cultural transformation requires leader self-awareness, vulnerability, intention, and long-term commitment.
- Real success lies in sustainable *commitment* rather than short-term control or compliance.
- Leaders must prepare for—and lead through—the unprecedented challenges posed by the *four ages*: environmental, technological, social, and digital.
- This model is not prescriptive but invites reflection, dialogue, and incremental application.
