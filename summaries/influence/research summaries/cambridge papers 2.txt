### Core Points

1. **Women’s Mate Preferences and Reproductive Strategies**  
   - Human reproduction imposes higher obligatory parental investment on women (gestation, lactation, rearing) compared to men, leading to evolved selectivity in mate choice (Parental Investment Theory).  
   - Women pursue both long-term mating (LTM) strategies emphasizing investment and protection, and short-term mating (STM) strategies focusing on good genes. Preference for these fluctuates with fertility status (e.g., ovulatory cycle).  
   - Women’s STM preferences involve physical cues (facial masculinity, body muscularity, facial symmetry, limbal rings indicating health), signaling good genes and immunocompetence.  
   - LTM preferences prioritize men’s willingness and ability to invest resources, display benevolence, and maintain fidelity, with cues including facial hair (beards), social status, and prosocial behaviors.  
   - Women’s interests fluctuate with environment and individual differences, adjusting preferences based on pathogen prevalence, resource availability, and personal traits like sociosexuality and personality.  

2. **Female Sexual Attraction and Competition Tactics**  
   - Women actively use tactics to attract and retain mates, including appearance enhancement, flirting, sexual availability signaling, and competitor derogation.  
   - Female intrasexual competition mainly manifests as appearance enhancement and indirect aggression rather than direct physical aggression due to evolutionary and social costs.  
   - Individual differences (e.g., Big Five traits, Dark Triad, sociosexuality) influence women’s attraction and competitive behaviors.  
   - New social environments, such as dating apps and social media, offer expanded arenas for mating competition and short-term mating opportunities, shaping modern tactics and motivations.  

3. **Extra-Pair Sexual Interest and Inﬁdelity**  
   - Extra-pair sexual interest (EPC) is an evolutionarily adaptive strategy for women and men, allowing women to secure good genes beyond their long-term mate, and men to maximize reproductive success.  
   - Women’s preferences for good genes (e.g., masculinity, symmetry, genetic dissimilarity) and extra-pair interest increase near ovulation, although recent meta-analyses show mixed evidence on the strength of these cyclical shifts.  
   - Environmental factors (sex ratios, resource scarcity, pathogen prevalence) modulate extra-pair sexual interest and mating behaviors.  
   - Extra-pair interest is influenced by personality traits, notably Dark Triad traits and sociosexuality, and entails substantial costs (risk of social condemnation, loss of primary partner, sexual victimization).  
   - Both men and women engage in mate poaching, with strategies varying by mating goals and affected by mate value differentials.  

4. **Copulatory Adaptations and Female Sexuality**  
   - Female copulatory behavior is active and complex rather than passive; female behaviors and genital physiology modulate mating success, sperm capture, and fertilization potential.  
   - Visual, tactile, olfactory, and auditory cues are important in copulatory interactions; genital anatomy and neurological mechanisms (e.g., lordosis behavior, pelvic contractions) support successful reproduction.  
   - Oral sex is an evolved sexual behavior observed cross-culturally and in various species, with functions including mate attraction, sperm competition, social bonding, and increased sexual pleasure.  
   - Female orgasm remains debated as adaptation or by-product, with substantial evidence now supporting it as an adaptation facilitating pair bonding, genetically advantageous mate choice, and increasing conception probability through physiological mechanisms.  

5. **Female Sexual Desire and Copulatory Urgency**  
   - Sexual desire in women is context-dependent, sensitive to age, relationship status, fertility, partner status, and sociocultural factors; women engage in sexual activity for emotional bonding, resources, and sexual pleasure.  
   - Women’s sexual desire fluctuates across the menstrual cycle; although the evidence on ovulatory shifts is mixed, desire generally rises around ovulation and is related to adaptive mating strategies.  
   - Partner’s qualities (status, fidelity, hygiene) and interpersonal communication influence women’s sexual desire and copulatory behavior.  
   - Excessively low or high sexual desire may be maladaptive or pathological; evolutionary mismatch and sociocultural factors can alter sexual motivation.  

6. **Emotional Commitment in Relationships**  
   - Emotional commitment is a psychological adaptation evolved for mate fidelity, parental investment, and reciprocal cooperation, involving love, trust, and mutual investment across the life course.  
   - Attachment theory explains dynamics of emotional commitment from infancy to adulthood, with adult romantic attachment styles linked to evolutionary strategies and life history theory.  
   - Individuals exhibit varying degrees of emotional commitment based on attachment avoidance and anxiety, with patterns shaped by early experiences but not determinative for adult attachment style.  
   - Emotional commitment facilitates biparental care, social bonding, and cooperative networks, including bonds with kin, friends, and companion animals.  

7. **Female Sexual Jealousy**  
   - Jealousy is a complex motivational state evolved to protect valued romantic relationships from potential rivals and threats to reproductive resources.  
   - Sexual jealousy (concern over sexual inﬁdelity) predominates in men, emotional jealousy (concern over emotional inﬁdelity and resource diversion) predominates in women, a pattern robust cross-culturally.  
   - Jealousy involves multiple cognitive, emotional, and behavioral components, including mate guarding and indirect aggression.  
   - Individual variation in jealousy is affected by self-esteem, personality traits (e.g., neuroticism, Dark Triad), developmental factors, and social context (e.g., operational sex ratios).  
   - Pathological jealousy involves excessive and maladaptive jealousy leading to psychological distress and potential violence, treatable via cognitive-behavioral therapy.  
   - Nonheterosexual individuals and those in consensually nonmonogamous relationships show different jealousy patterns.  

8. **Evolution of Shifts in Partner Attractiveness**  
   - Women’s attraction to partner traits signaling genetic quality fluctuates with fertility and reproductive stage (ovulation, pregnancy, menopause), while attraction to traits signaling status, resources, and commitment remains more stable.  
   - Transitions such as becoming a parent or aging may increase women’s attraction to cues of parental investment and companionship.  
   - Women calibrate their mate preferences and perceptions of partner qualities over time to maintain psychological consistency within relationships.  
   - Individual differences and sociocultural contexts influence the importance women ascribe to physical attractiveness, status, and intimacy in partners across the relationship life-course.  

9. **Evolutionary Psychological Explanation of Maternal Filicide**  
   - Maternal filicide (killing of children by mothers or stepmothers) is best understood as an extreme outcome of psychological mechanisms evolved to regulate discriminative maternal investment to maximize lifetime reproductive success.  
   - Reproductive conflicts arise when maternal investment in one child compromises the care for others or the woman’s future reproductive potential, especially toward neonates and when social support is lacking.  
   - Stepchildren are at higher risk owing to lack of genetic relatedness and often fewer social provisions.  
   - A subcategory of filicides is associated with severe psychopathology (e.g., psychosis, suicidal ideation), characterized by different patterns of victim age, motives, and method.  
   - Societal and legal factors (birth control, abortion, social support, laws prohibiting physical punishment) strongly modulate rates of maternal filicide.  

10. **Female Provision of Oral Sex**  
     - Oral sex is widespread, cross-culturally and across species, with functions including sexual pleasure, sperm competition, cryptic female choice, social bonding, and mate retention.  
     - Human oral sex has long historical and cross-cultural roots, challenging assumptions that it is recent or purely recreational.  
     - Women perform oral sex partly to please partners, maintain relationships, and increase sexual satisfaction.  
     - Oral sex is influenced by personality, sociosexuality, mate retention motivation, and social norms, and varies with motivation and relationship status.  
     - Oral sex contributes to sexual arousal and satisfaction, facilitating orgasm, but also carries health risks requiring education and protection.  

11. **Adaptive Value of Women’s Orgasm**  
     - Female orgasm shares similarities with male orgasm but exhibits greater variability and can be achieved by different physiological pathways.  
     - Theories diverge on whether female orgasm is a by-product of male orgasm or an adaptation.  
     - Evidence supports adaptive functions: enhancing pair-bonding, increasing conception probability via physiological effects, and enabling mate/sire choice favoring high-quality partners.  
     - Hormones such as oxytocin and testosterone play important roles in orgasm and sexual motivation.  
     - Female orgasm frequency correlates with partner traits, sexual satisfaction, and relationship quality, though causality and mechanisms require further study.  

12. **Copulatory Urgency and Female Sexual Desire**  
     - Sexual desire motivates sexual behavior and varies with contextual (age, fertility, relationship status, partner factors) and individual difference factors.  
     - Women show context-dependent sexual desire that can fluctuate with menstrual cycle phase, partner attractiveness, and life circumstances (e.g., parenthood).  
     - Sexual desire is differentiated from arousal and involves complex neuroendocrine, psychological, and social processes.  
     - Deficits and excesses in sexual desire can be maladaptive or pathological, with evolutionary mismatch and sociocultural factors playing roles.  

13. **Postcopulatory Adaptations and Mate Retention**  
     - Mate retention behaviors aim to prevent partner’s inﬁdelity or desertion and are selected adaptively to maintain beneficial long-term mating arrangements.  
     - Women generally engage more in appearance enhancement for mate retention, men more in resource display and direct guarding.  
     - Mate retention efforts vary with mate value, relationship satisfaction, personality, emotionality, and cultural context.  
     - Emotional mechanisms such as jealousy and love underlie mate retention efforts and are modulated by evolved sex differences and individual differences.  

14. **Shifts in Female Partner Attractiveness and Preference Across the Life Course**  
     - Female preferences for male partner traits signaling direct reproductive beneﬁts (good genes) shift with fertility cycle, contraceptive use, pregnancy, and menopause.  
     - Female preferences for long-term partner traits (commitment, parental ability, companionship) are more stable but also shift with transition to parenthood and aging.  
     - Women calibrate their mate preferences and perceptions of partner traits over the life course for psychological consistency and reproductive benefit.  

15. **Female Sexual Jealousy and Mate Guarding**  
     - Jealousy protects valued romantic relationships from rival threats; men focus on sexual inﬁdelity, women on emotional inﬁdelity.  
     - Jealousy triggers mate guarding behaviors by the “owned” partner and deters rivals by derogation and indirect aggression.  
     - Female mate guarding behaviors include appearance enhancement and sexual behavior; indirect aggression can be social exclusion and gossip.  
     - Jealousy is affected by individual differences (personality, attachment), environmental conditions (operational sex ratio), and developmental history.  

16. **Emotional Commitment and Attachment Theory from an Evolutionary Perspective**  
     - Attachment is an evolved behavioral system designed to promote infants’ survival by seeking close proximity to caregivers and forms the basis of relationships across the lifespan.  
     - Attachment styles in adults (avoidance, anxiety) vary individually and show modest sex differences aligned with evolutionary life-history theory.  
     - Measurement of attachment includes behavioral observations, interviews, self-reports, and narrative methods; integrating these approaches is valuable.  
     - Emotional commitment facilitates bonding and cooperation in romantic and kin relationships, critical for biparental care, social support, and ultimately reproductive fitness.  
     - Attachment styles and emotional commitment impact relationship quality and an individual’s social and psychological well-being.  

**Important Details**  
- Sex differences often reflect differing evolutionary pressures faced by men and women related to parental investment, paternity certainty, and reproductive costs.  
- Environmental factors such as operational sex ratio, pathogen prevalence, social inequality, and resources modulate female mate preferences and intrasexual competition.  
- Life history theory explains individual differences in mating behaviors as strategies adapted to environmental stability and predictability.  
- Sexual receptivity and motivation in humans are temporally variable, affected by endocrine changes, attachment, partner characteristics, and sociocultural factors.  
- Female intrasexual competition entails self-promotion and competitor derogation tactics moderated by social consequences and biological constraints.  
- Evolutionary perspectives explain female filicide as a strategy of discriminant maternal investment rather than purely psychopathology; however, pathological cases exist.  
- Oral sex and female orgasm research suggest evolved roles beyond reproduction, including mate retention, sexual satisfaction, sperm competition, mate choice, and pair-bonding.  
- Jealousy has multiple dimensions and triggers distinct male and female behavioral strategies for mate retention and rivalry deterrence.

This synthesis draws from empirical, theoretical, and cross-disciplinary work, presenting a nuanced, dynamic, and integrated portrait of female sexual adaptations, strategies, and psychosocial processes within an evolutionary framework.

#### 1. Introduction: Evolutionary Perspectives on Female Sexuality and Adaptations

Female sexual adaptations involve complex psychological, behavioral, and physiological mechanisms shaped by evolutionary pressures related to reproduction and mate selection. Given women's greater obligatory parental investment—pregnancy, lactation, and offspring care (Trivers, 1972)—their mating strategies emphasize **mate quality**, in terms of genetic fitness and partner investment willingness. Women exhibit **precopulatory** (mate choice and attraction), **copulatory** (sexual behavior and physiology), and **postcopulatory** adaptations (emotional commitment and mate retention). Understanding these adaptations provides insights into preferences, competition, sexual behavior, and relationship maintenance unique to human females.

Key notions include:
- **Parental Investment Theory:** The sex investing more in offspring is choosier and faces higher reproductive costs.
- **Operational Sex Ratio (OSR):** The locally available mates ratio influences female mating tactics.
- **Sexual Strategies Theory:** Women prioritize long-term mating goals (LTM) for resource access and biparental investment, but short-term mating goals (STM) may occur for genetic benefits.
- **Intrasexual Competition:** Female-to-female rivalry focused substantially on physical attractiveness and behavioral strategies.
- **Intersexual Selection:** Female mate preferences actively shape male traits and behaviors.

#### 2. Female Intrasexual Competition

- **Definition:** Competition among females for access to desirable males, seen across cultures and evolutionary history.
- **Critical Drivers:** Scarce high-quality mates and OSR biases prompt women to enhance attractiveness, use **self-promotion tactics** (cosmetics, appearance, sexuality), and rely on **indirect aggression** (gossip, social exclusion) to limit rival success.
- **Attractiveness and Mate Value:** Women with lower **mate value** (self or perceived) display more intrasexual aggression and exploitative tactics; high mate value affords greater selectivity.
- **Appearance Enhancement:** From makeup and tanning to plastic surgery and costly resources (luxury goods), women modulate their sexual appeal to attract and retain mates.
- **Gossip and Social Networks:** Gossip serves as a mate retention and rivalry tool, aimed at damaging rivals’ reputations but packaged as concern for social harmony.
- **Human uniqueness:** Unlike other species, humans employ elaborate indirect strategies; social bonds remain essential for balancing cooperation and competition.
- **Environmental and individual variation:** Competition intensity in women rises with resource scarcity, pathogen prevalence, physical threats, and individual personality (Big Five, sociosexuality).
- **Impact of sexual orientation:** Lesbian and bisexual women also engage in rivalry but display particular patterns distinct from heterosexual women.

#### 3. Female Intersexual Selection and Mate Preferences

- **Intersexual Selection:** Female choice drives the evolution of male secondary sexual characteristics, balancing **good genes** (physical health, masculinity, symmetry) and **investment potential** (resource access, prosociality).
- **Male Traits Favored by Females:**
  - **Physical:** Youth, health indicators, facial symmetry, masculinity, muscularity.
  - **Behavioral:** Kindness, trustworthiness, commitment, parenting ability.
  - **Social:** Wealth, status, protection capability.
- **Shifts in Female Preferences:**
  - **Across ovulatory cycle:** Heightened preference for masculinity, symmetry, dominance, and good genes near fertility peak to optimize genetic benefits.
  - **Hormonal contraception:** Use suppresses ovulation and reduces preferences for masculine traits.
  - **Pregnancy:** Increased attraction to traits signaling health and immunocompetence.
  - **Menopause and Aging:** Shifting attraction toward longevity and companionship traits over genetic quality.
- **Individual differences:** Women with higher self-perceived mate value have stronger mate preferences; personality traits and life history strategies modulate preferences and mating effort.
- **Female mate poaching:** Women also compete for men by trying to lure partners away, with success linked to own personality and mate value.
- **Mate retention as adaptive behavior:** Women use verbal, emotional, and physical tactics to maintain valued long-term partners.

#### 4. Copulatory Adaptations

- **Active Female Role:** Females are not passive in sexual reproduction; behaviors such as **lordosis posture** (arching back to expose genitalia), **copulatory thrusting**, and **vaginal-lubrication** are essential for sperm capture.
- **Sensory Cues:** Somatosensory input, vaginal/urethral stimulation, and orgasmic contractions facilitate copulation; females can modulate these to optimize reproductive success.
- **Postcopulatory Choice:** Women may influence sperm transport and fertilization via cryptic choice mechanisms.
- **Variation Across Species:** Human copulation shares similarities with other mammals but is unique in degree of behavioral flexibility and conscious modulation.
- **Oral Sex:**
  - Documented in many species and historically cross-cultural in humans.
  - Serves multiple functions: sexual pleasure, mate retention, good genes detection, and inﬁdelity avoidance.
  - Modern prevalence is high worldwide and socially influenced.
- **Sexual Fantasies:** Reflect deep motivational forces linked to reproductive success; sex differences in content and frequency exist, with women fantasizing less about multiple partners and more about commitment.
- **Orgasm:** Female orgasm is argued to be an adaptation related to mate choice, reproductive success, pair bonding, and sexual satisfaction; not a mere by-product of male orgasm.

#### 5. Sexual Desire and Copulatory Urgency

- **Evolutionary-Developmental View:** Women’s sexual desire is context-dependent and dynamically calibrated to maximize reproductive success.
- **Age and Sexual Desire:** Desire declines with age, but is also affected by partner status, relationship quality, and external environmental factors.
- **Parental Care Trade-off:** Having young children leads women to decrease sexual desire, shifting effort toward offspring survival.
- **Relationship Factors:** Communication ability, partner status, and hygiene influence desire.
- **Excessive Desire:** Exceedingly high desire can lead to dysfunction and is conceptualized as an **evolutionary mismatch** under modern conditions.
- **Psychopathology:** The modern medical model of “disorder” may pathologize adaptive mechanisms.
- **Testosterone and Desire:** Hormonal influences are complex and context-dependent.

#### 6. Emotional Commitment

- **Foundational Role:** Emotional commitment is an evolved psychological state motivating long-term pair bonding to maximize inclusive fitness via biparental care.
- **Neurological and Hormonal Bases:** Oxytocin, dopamine, and brain reward centers are involved.
- **Love as the Basis:** Romantic love and commitment often go hand in hand and promote relationship longevity.
- **Sex Differences:** Women tend to initiate declarations of love later, less frequently, and men more.

#### 7. Female Sexual Jealousy

- **Definition:** Complex, evolved emotion emerging when valued relationships are threatened by a rival.
- **Types:** Emotional, sexual, anxious, reactive, preventive jealousy.
- **Universal Sex Differences:** Women are more distressed by emotional inﬁdelity (loss of resources, abandonment risk); men more by sexual inﬁdelity (paternity uncertainty).
- **Individual Differences:** Anxiety, neuroticism, self-esteem, sociosexuality modulate jealousy levels.
- **Cross-cultural and cross-species evidence:** Support the universality of jealousy with social context modulation.
- **Neuroscience:** Sex-speciﬁc brain activation patterns linked to jealousy.
- **Functions:** Motivates mate guarding, competitor derogation, and indirect aggression.
- **Same-sex jealousy:** Present but distinct mechanisms in heterosexuals vs. homosexuals.
- **Hormonal inﬂuences:** Cycle-linked variation observed.
- **Pathological jealousy:** Clinical syndrome linked to psychiatric disorders, treatable by cognitive behavioral therapy.

#### 8. Maternal Filicide

- **Core Paradox:** Mothers killing their offspring is a reproductive conundrum but can be understood via **reproductive conﬂict theory**.
- **Reproductive Conﬂict:** Maternal investment trade-offs exist because resources are limited; investment in one child may limit potential investment in others.
- **Adaptive logic:** Maternal ﬁlicide can be (a) an extreme manifestation of evolved discriminant parental investment mechanisms (epiphenomenon) or (b) a direct adaptive strategy (rare).
- **Subcategories:**
  - Genetic mothers vs. stepmothers: stepmothers more likely to perpetrate lethal abuse/neglect due to lack of genetic relatedness and reproductive incentive.
  - Mothers with psychopathology (e.g., psychosis, suicidal ideation): motivation, method, and victim characteristics differ.
- **Age of child and mother:** Younger children and younger mothers pose higher risk contexts for maternal ﬁlicide.
- **Social and cultural factors:** Support for mothers (alloparents), legality of abandonment, and social equity in parenthood modulate rates.
- **Behavioral mechanisms:** Lower investment, neglect, abuse, abandonment, and direct violence.
- **Evolutionary understanding aids prevention and contextualizes the taboo.
