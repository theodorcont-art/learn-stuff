Core Points

1. Human relations and interpersonal skills are critical to success and happiness in both personal and professional life, often outweighing technical knowledge.  
2. The cornerstone principles in dealing with people are respect, understanding, appreciation, tact, and the ability to inspire enthusiasm.  
3. Criticism, condemnation, and complaining are ineffective and damaging methods of influencing others because they arouse resentment and resistance rather than change.  
4. Genuine appreciation and honest praise are profoundly motivating forces that improve relationships and inspire better performance.  
5. The deepest human desire is to feel important and appreciated; recognizing this craving and honoring it sincerely unlocks influence over others.  
6. Most people are primarily interested in themselves, their ambitions, and desires; successful influence requires seeing the world from their point of view and making the other person’s wants central to communication.  
7. Arguments and direct confrontations about being right or wrong rarely change opinions and often generate antagonism; avoiding arguments or engaging them tactfully is more effective.  
8. Admitting one’s own mistakes frankly and promptly disarms hostility and encourages reciprocal honesty.  
9. Asking questions, encouraging others to talk about themselves, and listening attentively builds rapport and gives influence more easily than talking about oneself.  
10. Dramatizing one’s ideas and using engaging, vivid presentations captures attention and creates enthusiasm.  
11. People should be gently challenged and given meaningful responsibilities or titles in order to motivate and empower them to improve.  
12. Positive reinforcement, encouragement, and making faults seem easy to correct are more likely to produce lasting change and cooperation than reprimands or threats.  
13. Helping others save face and avoid embarrassment preserves dignity and maintains goodwill, which is essential for effective leadership and persuasion.  
14. Human nature is consistent across time and culture: people respond best to kindness, respect, and genuine interest rather than aggression or coercion.  
15. Continuous review, practice, and application of these principles are necessary for habitual effective human relations; the book encourages sustained effort for results.

Key Conclusions

1. Effective communication and leadership depend not on force or argument, but on understanding the other person’s perspective, making them feel important, and appealing to their nobler motives.  
2. The acquisition of social skills such as sincere appreciation, tactful persuasion, attentive listening, and the ability to arouse enthusiasm can profoundly transform lives, careers, and relationships.  
3. Avoiding unnecessary criticism and instead fostering a positive climate through praise and encouragement leads to improved morale, cooperation, and performance.  
4. People are more inclined to change their opinions or behavior when they feel valued, respected, and not humiliated, underscoring the importance of emotional intelligence in human relations.  
5. The habit of arguing and confrontation is largely counterproductive; it breeds resistance and isolates the arguer. Instead, engaging others with empathy and starting conversations with points of agreement is more productive.  
6. Humility in admitting mistakes and tactful acknowledgment of others’ viewpoints foster trust and openness. Leaders who can do this gain respect and influence.  
7. The principle of “making the other person happy to do what you want” by highlighting mutual benefits is an essential tool in negotiation, sales, and collaborative efforts.  
8. Consistent practice of principles such as smiling, remembering names, and showing genuine interest creates positive impressions that open doors in, social and business settings.  
9. The universal and timeless nature of these human behavioral truths reinforces their relevance today, transcending culture, profession, and era.  
10. The power of influence is not manipulation but earned by sincere concern, creating an atmosphere where others willingly align with your ideas or requests.  
11. Dale Carnegie’s methods and courses remain effective due to their practical approach to addressing human needs for recognition, appreciation, and belonging.  
12. Practicing these principles requires determination and persistence but results in rich, lasting benefits, including personal happiness, professional success, and smoother human interactions.

### Preface to Revised Edition  
Originally published in 1937 with unexpectedly large success, *How to Win Friends and Influence People* filled a deep human need across generations and languages. The revision sought to update examples and names, refresh dated expressions without changing core content, and adapt to contemporary social contexts. The revision maintains Dale Carnegie's conversational style and practical approach, aiming to continue inspiring readers globally.  

### How This Book Was Written — And Why by Dale Carnegie  
Carnegie recounts his journey starting in 1912 teaching public speaking, gradually realizing skills in human relations were even more crucial. Research highlighted that technical knowledge accounts for roughly 15% of financial success, while 85% is due to “human engineering” - personal skills, leadership, and personality. Despite recognition that “the ability to deal with people” is the top commodity (citing Rockefeller), few educational courses have addressed this need. Carnegie thus set out to write a practical manual based on extensive research, interviews with prominent individuals, and classroom experiments where adults tested principles and reported transformational results. He stresses this book is about action, not just theory, and advocates persistent application and review to form new habits.  

Key points for readers:  
- Develop a deep desire to improve interpersonal skills.  
- Read chapters multiple times, thinking about practical application.  
- Mark important ideas and review monthly.  
- Practically use principles regularly and self-check progress critically.  
- Make learning interactive, including companions aiding by monitoring your adherence to principles.  

### Part One: Fundamental Techniques in Handling People

#### 1. Don’t Criticize, Condemn or Complain  
Carnegie opens with vivid stories of criminals (“Two Gun” Crowley, Al Capone, Dutch Schultz) who rationalize or entirely reject blame for their actions. He shows this common human trait extends to everyone: people rarely admit fault and instead defend themselves and resist criticism. Criticism is futile because it puts people on the defensive, wounds pride, and provokes resentment, often worsening behavior. Psychological studies show reward encourages learning much more effectively than punishment.  

Illustrations include:  
- Safety coordinator changing approach from authoritative commands to kindly explanations, resulting in better compliance without resentment.  
- Historic and contemporary examples where criticism failed (e.g., Theodore Roosevelt vs. Taft political split, Teapot Dome scandal) because people refuse to accept blame or corrected viewpoints.  
- Abraham Lincoln’s approach: despite many reasons to criticize his generals during the Civil War, Lincoln exercised charity and rarely rebuked them publicly, understanding criticism breeds defensiveness.  
- Mark Twain’s funny but unsent letters show cathartic expression of criticism often better written than said.  

Carnegie contrasts this with the art of understanding. He emphasizes:  
- People are creatures motivated by emotions, pride, and vanity, not logic.  
- Criticism is dangerous and counters productive relationships.  
- Sympathy and forgiveness breed improved relations and results.  
- Tactfully avoid criticism or recognize limitations in yourself before others.  

**Principle 1:** Don’t criticize, condemn or complain.  

#### 2. The Big Secret of Dealing with People  
The single method to get others to do things is to *make them want to do it*. This is accomplished by appealing to their deepest desires, chief among which is the innate craving for importance and appreciation (citing Freud, John Dewey, William James). People’s primary drive is to feel important, comparable to fundamental needs like food and sleep.  

- This desire to be important motivates good or bad behavior (e.g., Rockefeller’s philanthropy vs. Dillinger’s crime).  
- Historical and anecdotal examples show how great leaders knew this truth and used it to motivate others.  
- Importance explains human behavior in social, professional, and even pathological contexts.  
- Sincere, honest appreciation is critical to fulfilling this craving and making people cooperative.  

**Principle 2:** Give honest and sincere appreciation.  

#### 3. He Who Can Do This Has the Whole World with Him; He Who Cannot Walks a Lonely Way  
Carnegie uses the fishing analogy: to catch fish, you bait the hook according to what the fish want, not what you want. Similarly, to influence people, you must talk about what they want and how to obtain it.  

- Examples: appealing to customers’ desires, children’s wants (Tommy wanting a tricycle), business negotiations and letters written in terms of the other person’s interests.  
- Getting others to perceive benefit aligned with their needs causes willing cooperation.  
- Sales success typically comes from focusing on customer wants, not your own agenda.  
- Knowing and acting on the other person’s perspective always improves interpersonal effectiveness.  

**Principle 3:** Arouse in the other person an eager want.  

### Part Two: Ways to Make People Like You

#### 1. Do This and You’ll Be Welcome Anywhere  
Genuine interest in other people is the key to being liked. Unlike humans who are self-focused, a dog’s unconditional and visible affection makes it universally loved. Humans crave appreciation and interest from others.  

- People don’t naturally want to hear about you; they want to talk about themselves and their lives.  
- Examples of major figures (Theodore Roosevelt, Franklin Roosevelt, magicians) show genuine interest in others as a pathway to success in relationships.  
- Remembering birthdays, sincerely complimenting, showing enthusiasm for others’ interests, even on the phone, builds warmth and loyalty.  
- Flattery is not genuine appreciation and is universally disliked. Genuine praise comes from the heart, noticing real qualities.  

**Principle 1:** Become genuinely interested in other people.  

#### 2. A Simple Way to Make a Good First Impression  
Smile sincerely. A smile is a powerful nonverbal cue expressing liking and goodwill. It encourages warmth and improves interactions instantly, even on the telephone.  

- Smiling is linked to better communication, sales, and relationships.  
- Even when unseen, a smile influences the tone of one’s voice and impacts listener’s perception positively.  
- Smiling can transform home, work, and social atmosphere.  
- Author shares own story of learning to use continuous smiling with compelling results.  

**Principle 2:** Smile.  

#### 3. If You Don’t Do This, You Are Headed for Trouble  
Remembering and using a person’s name is critical to winning friendship and cooperation. A name represents identity and uniqueness, and people take great pride in their own name—even more than other personal attributes.  

- Historical examples: Rockefeller naming mills after clients, Carnegie’s naming of rabbits, and personal stories of using names to charm and help.  
- Not remembering names or mispronouncing them puts one at a disadvantage.  
- A name, used in conversation, can warm the heart and open doors.  

**Principle 3:** Remember that a person’s name is to that person the sweetest and most important sound in any language.  

#### 4. An Easy Way to Become a Good Conversationalist  
Listen attentively. People prefer to talk about themselves and their interests. By encouraging others to talk, asking questions, and showing genuine interest, you become a better conversationalist without dominating the conversation.  

- Examples include listening to experts or friends on any subject and encouraging rapt attention and warmth.  
- Listening is an appreciated compliment, building rapport and trust.  
- Interrupting or dominating conversations often alienates others.  

**Principle 4:** Be a good listener. Encourage others to talk about themselves.  

#### 5. How to Interest People  
Talk about what matters to the other person. Explore what interests your interlocutor, express genuine appreciation for it, and let them share stories and experiences on their own terms.  

- Examples: politicians, salespeople, managers seeking alignment with the other's concerns to win cooperation.  
- Colonel House’s subtle persuasion of President Wilson, where ideas become the listener’s own, is instructive.  
- Engage others by asking about their passions, hobbies, or values — this nurtures connection.  

**Principle 5:** Talk in terms of the other person’s interests.  

#### 6. How to Make People Like You Instantly  
Give honest, unexpected, specific compliments relating to the other person. There is power in sincere, spontaneous appreciation that makes people feel important and valued.  

- Stories reflect how small praise about things such as hair, dress, or skill can transform moods and relationships.  
- Flattering others sincerely elevates their opinion of themselves, generating goodwill instantly.  

**Principle 6:** Make the other person feel important—and do it sincerely.  

### Part Three: How to Win People to Your Way of Thinking

#### 1. You Can’t Win an Argument  
Arguments are rarely won; they usually end with both sides more convinced of their own rightness, but resentment and ill-will remain. Even if you "win," you lose the other person’s goodwill and cooperation.  

- Lincoln and Wilson avoided direct confrontation to maintain unity and effectiveness.  
- Evidence shows people are emotional creatures influenced more by feeling than facts.  
- Better to avoid arguments entirely or approach differences with humility and tact.  

**Principle 1:** The only way to get the best of an argument is to avoid it.  

#### 2. A Sure Way of Making Enemies - And How to Avoid It  
Show respect for others’ opinions and never say "You’re wrong." Use diplomacy even when confident you are right.  

- Historical examples demonstrate diplomatic success vs. alienation by bluntness.  
- Letters and business interactions succeed more often when addressing the other person’s viewpoint respectfully and framing requests as mutual benefit.  

**Principle 2:** Show respect for the other person’s opinions. Never say, "You’re wrong."  

#### 3. If You’re Wrong, Admit It  
Admitting your mistakes quickly and emphatically disarms conflict and earns respect. Many historical and modern examples (Lincoln, Franklin, business anecdotes) show that acknowledging fault builds rapport and trust.  

- It is easier to admit faults than to be condemned by others.  
- Self-criticism can transform hostile situations into cooperative ones.  
- Humility strengthens influence far more than defensiveness.  

**Principle 3:** If you are wrong, admit it quickly and emphatically.  

#### 4. Begin in a Friendly Way  
Hostility breeds hostility. Begin discussions or requests with warmth and friendliness to promote positive outcomes.  

- Stories of Rockefeller calming labor strikes and G.E. showing sympathy to employees illustrate this.  
- Kindness disarms opposition and helps open dialogue.  

**Principle 4:** Begin in a friendly way.  

#### 5. Get the Other Person Saying Yes, Yes Immediately  
Start conversations emphasizing agreement, common goals, and shared values to create a positive atmosphere. The psychological momentum of agreement increases cooperation.  

- Avoid early “No” responses which harden opposition.  
- Use leading questions that get affirmative responses to build mutual agreement gradually.  

**Principle 5:** Get the other person saying “yes, yes” immediately.  

#### 6. Let the Other Person Do a Great Deal of the Talking  
Encourage others to express themselves fully. People like to be heard, and they often discover faults or solutions when given the chance to talk.  

- Listening builds trust and allows you to understand motivations and objections better.  

**Principle 6:** Let the other person do a great deal of the talking.  

#### 7. Let the Other Person Feel That the Idea Is His or Hers  
People prefer to embrace ideas they have “discovered” themselves. Present your suggestions as questions or possibilities and let the counterpart develop conclusions that align with your goals.  

- This increases ownership and reduces resistance.  

**Principle 7:** Let the other person feel that the idea is his or hers.  

#### 8. Try Honestly to See Things from the Other Person’s Point of View  
Empathy is critical to understanding objections and relating effectively. Realize that others’ attitudes and behaviors have reasons grounded in their perspective and experiences.  

- Tactics effective in business, family, and cross-cultural situations require seeing the world as the other person does.  

**Principle 8:** Try honestly to see things from the other person’s point of view.  

#### 9. Be Sympathetic with the Other Person’s Ideas and Desires  
Sympathy helps neutralize hostility and build connection. Express understanding and validate feelings or concerns, even if you disagree.  

- This accords dignity, diffuses conflict, and paves the way for persuasion.  

**Principle 9:** Be sympathetic with the other person’s ideas and desires.  

#### 10. Appeal to the Nobler Motives  
Assume people act with honorable motives and appeal to values such as fairness, pride, responsibility, or love. People prefer to act in ways consistent with their ideal self-image.  

- Examples from Rockefeller, notable leaders, and humane appeals generate cooperation beyond self-interest.  

**Principle 10:** Appeal to the nobler motives.  

#### 11. Dramatize Your Ideas  
Present arguments and ideas vividly and imaginatively to captivate attention and create emotional impact. Use analogies, demonstrations, visual aids, stories, or surprise to enhance persuasion.  

- Theatrical approaches and dramatization make concepts memorable and convincing.  

**Principle 11:** Dramatize your ideas.  

#### 12. Throw Down a Challenge  
People are motivated by challenges and the opportunity to demonstrate ability and courage. Provoking competitive spirit or a shared goal elicits best efforts.  

- Historical and business examples show inspired performance when a challenge is issued.  

**Principle 12:** Throw down a challenge.  

### Part Four: Be a Leader: How to Change People Without Giving Offense or Arousing Resentment  

#### 1. If You Must Find Fault, This Is the Way to Begin  
Start by sincere praise and appreciation before addressing errors. This softens resistance and makes people receptive to correction.  

- Examples: Coolidge’s compliment before correcting a secretary’s punctuation, McKinley’s tactful public speech revisions, and Lincoln’s diplomatic rebuke to generals.  

**Principle 1:** Begin with praise and honest appreciation.  

#### 2. How to Criticize — And Not Be Hated for It   
Give criticism indirectly, avoid blunt condemnation, and use kindness, presents, or gentle requests to correct mistakes.  

- Historical and contemporary examples prove indirectness breeds cooperation and avoids resentment.  

**Principle 2:** Call attention to people’s mistakes indirectly.  

#### 3. Talk About Your Own Mistakes First  
Before criticizing others, admit your own faults and mistakes to create rapport and reduce defensiveness.  

- Philosopher Benjamin Franklin’s autobiography exemplifies this.  
- Business anecdotes confirm improved relationships and results.  

**Principle 3:** Talk about your mistakes before criticizing the other person.  

#### 4. Ask Questions Instead of Giving Direct Orders  
Request cooperation by asking questions that imply choice and respect, rather than dictating commands, to enhance motivation.  

- Asking questions stimulates initiative and avoids resentment.  

**Principle 4:** Ask questions instead of giving direct orders.  

#### 5. Let the Other Person Save Face  
Help others preserve pride and dignity by avoiding blunt humiliation. Allow people to maintain self-respect even when corrected or replaced.  

- Examples: G.E. tailored Steinmetz’s job title to save pride, tactful employee terminations, and diplomatic redirection of criticism into constructive conversations.  

**Principle 5:** Let the other person save face.  

#### 6. Praise the Slightest Improvement and Praise Every Improvement  
Encourage repeatedly and sincerely, highlighting every gain. This reinforces positive behavior and motivates ongoing improvement.  

- Positive reinforcement outperforms criticism in creating change.  

**Principle 6:** Praise every improvement. Be "hearty in your approbation and lavish in your praise.”  

#### 7. Give the Other Person a Fine Reputation to Live Up To  
Impress others with a positive image of their character or abilities, causing them to live up to elevated expectations.  

- Use reputation as motivation; confer titles, responsibility, and trust to inspire performance.  

**Principle 7:** Give the other person a fine reputation to live up to.  

#### 8. Use Encouragement. Make the Fault Seem Easy to Correct  
Focus on encouragement, optimism, and the possibility of improvement rather than criticism or faultfinding. This sustains morale and promotes self-efficacy.  

**Principle 8:** Use encouragement; make the fault seem easy to correct.  

#### 9. Make the Other Person Happy About Doing the Thing You Suggest  
Frame requests or directives so the other person feels satisfied and personally benefited, increasing cooperation.  

- Appealing to self-interest and using tactful framing helps avoid resistance.  

**Principle 9:** Make the other person happy about doing what you suggest.

### Summary of Dale Carnegie’s Life and Legacy (by Lowell Thomas)  
Dale Carnegie rose from humble Missouri farm-boy origins, overcoming poverty and obstacles by developing public speaking skills and an understanding of human nature. He perfected a popular adult education course combining self-confidence building, effective speaking, and human relations. Carnegie’s empirical, practical approach helped thousands around the world develop courage, influence, and interpersonal skills, reshaping adult education and leadership training. His books and courses remain seminal in personal development fields, teaching timeless principles of connection, persuasion, and leadership rooted in empathy and respect.
